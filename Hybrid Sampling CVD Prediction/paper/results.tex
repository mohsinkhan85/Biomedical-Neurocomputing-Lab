The results are presented in eight parts. Section~\ref{sec:resB} evaluates
the baseline HS pipeline on real, held-out records, and
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} identifies the design choices that limit its
performance. Section~\ref{sec:improvedres} presents the improved pipeline
with bootstrap confidence intervals, and Section~\ref{sec:cv} confirms its
results with five-fold cross-validation. Section~\ref{sec:riskfactors}
describes the risk factors and Section~\ref{sec:calibration} the
calibration of the predicted risks. Section~\ref{sec:compare} compares the findings with
existing studies, including the external evaluation on clinical data, and
Section~\ref{sec:limits} discusses the limitations. All experiments use a
fixed random state of 42 and can be reproduced with the released code.

\subsection{Baseline HS Pipeline on Real, Unseen Records}
\label{sec:resB}

Table~\ref{tab:protoB} (left) shows the HS models trained on the resampled
training set and evaluated on the 6,000 real records that were held out
before sampling. They reach only 57.4--70.8\% accuracy and an AUC of
0.57--0.81. The fully grown DT is close to chance level (AUC 0.574): it
memorises the training records, many of them duplicates, and these do not
recur among new patients. All HS models are also very
conservative on real data: their recall ranges from 18.5\% (DT) to 60.2\%
(SVM), so most CVD cases are missed. This follows from the changes that HS
makes to the training data (Section~\ref{sec:hs}): ENN keeps only the most
clear-cut heart disease cases (38.1\% with a past stroke, compared with
16.4\% among the real training records), so the models learn that only such profiles
are positive and classify most real patients with less typical answers as
negative.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

To identify which design choices limit performance on real data, we varied
them one at a time for XGBoost and the DT (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The
AUC measures how well a model ranks patients by risk, independently of the
threshold; accuracy is reported both at the default threshold of 0.5 and at
the threshold tuned on the validation set.

\small
\begin{tabular}{llccc}

Model & Configuration & AUC (\%) & Acc.\ @ 0.5 (\%) & Acc.\ @ tuned (\%) \\
\midrule

\midrule

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Four findings emerge. First, hybrid sampling is the main cause of the loss:
replacing it with ROS alone raises the AUC of XGBoost from 0.743 to 0.821,
and dropping resampling altogether gives the same AUC (0.823; difference
from HS $+$7.9 percentage points, 95\% CI 6.9--8.9), showing that
synthetic records and ENN cleaning distort the data rather than add
information. Second, without resampling the default threshold of 0.5 is
unsuitable for imbalanced data (53.3\% accuracy, because almost every
record is predicted negative), whereas a threshold tuned on the validation
set restores 74.6\% accuracy; resampling and threshold tuning address the
same problem, but tuning does so without altering the data. Class weights
address it as well: with them, the default threshold is already close to the
tuned one (76.18\% vs.\ 76.00\% accuracy for XGBoost), so in the improved
pipeline threshold tuning mainly guards against a miscalibrated model. Third, using all
21 features instead of the nine correlation-selected ones increases the AUC
from 0.823 to 0.835 ($+$1.3 percentage points, 95\% CI 0.8--1.8): features such as sex and income carry predictive
information that a univariate correlation ranking misses
(Section~\ref{sec:riskfactors}).
Fourth, regularisation (shallow, early-stopped boosting and a depth-limited
tree) prevents the models from memorising individual records; together
with the other changes, it turns the DT from a near-random classifier into a
competitive model (AUC 0.825).

\subsection{Improved Pipeline}
\label{sec:improvedres}

Table~\ref{tab:protoB} (right) shows that the improved pipeline raises
every model on real, unseen records, and Fig.~\ref{fig:improved}
summarises its results. Accuracy
increases to 74.9--76.0\%, recall to 78.8--82.8\% and the AUC to
0.825--0.840. The largest gains concern recall: the improved XGBoost
detects 82.8\% of CVD cases, compared with 30.5\% for the baseline HS
XGBoost, and the improved DT detects 78.9\% compared with 18.5\%.
Table~\ref{tab:ci} gives 95\% bootstrap CIs: every gain in AUC and recall
is statistically significant, with CIs of the differences far from zero
(for example, $+$9.7 AUC points, 95\% CI 8.6--10.7, for XGBoost). The ROC
curves of all improved models nearly coincide (Fig.~\ref{fig:rocB}). The
improved XGBoost does not differ significantly from the NN and the MLP;
its advantage over LR and the SVM (0.3 AUC points) is statistically
significant but of no practical relevance, so logistic regression performs
on par with XGBoost and the three DL models.

\subsection{Five-Fold Cross-Validation}
\label{sec:cv}

To check that these results do not depend on one particular split, the
improved models were evaluated with stratified five-fold cross-validation
on the 223,781 real records that are not in the test set. In every fold,
20\% of the training portion was set aside for early stopping and threshold
tuning, and the held-out fold kept the natural prevalence of 9.3\%.
Table~\ref{tab:cv} reports the mean and standard deviation over the folds.
The AUC (0.824--0.839) and recall (78.9--82.5\%) agree with the test-set
results and vary little between folds (standard deviation of the AUC at
most 0.64 percentage points), confirming that the estimates are stable.
Accuracy (70.8--72.2\%) is lower than on the balanced test set because the
tuned threshold trades specificity for sensitivity and negatives make up
about 91\% of each fold.

Precision and F1 are much lower in cross-validation than on the balanced
test set because the folds keep the real prevalence. At a prevalence of
10\%, even a model with about 80\% sensitivity and 70\% specificity flags
roughly three to four healthy respondents for every true CVD case
(precision about 22\%). This is an inherent property of screening for a
condition of low prevalence, not a defect of a particular model, and it
should guide how such models are used: as inexpensive pre-screening tools
that refer at-risk individuals for clinical assessment, not as diagnostic
tests.

\subsection{Risk Factors}
\label{sec:riskfactors}

Table~\ref{tab:or} lists the adjusted odds ratios of all 21 survey
variables. The strongest associations with CVD are a history of stroke
(OR 2.61, 95\% CI 2.47--2.76), male sex (2.13), high cholesterol (1.84),
a cholesterol check in the past five years (1.81), high blood pressure
(1.67), poorer general health (1.60 per level), smoking (1.41), difficulty
walking (1.34) and age (1.28 per five-year category). These factors agree
with those identified on the same data by Deng et al.~\cite{deng} (age,
smoking, diabetes, hypertension and high cholesterol). Two of them, sex and
the cholesterol check, are not among the nine features selected by
correlation, which explains why using all features improves discrimination.
Conversely, BMI, days of poor physical health and health care coverage are
not significantly associated with CVD once the other variables are taken
into account.

Some associations run against intuition: heavy alcohol consumption
(OR 0.72) and higher income (0.97 per level) are associated with lower odds,
and physical activity and daily vegetable consumption with slightly higher
odds. Because BRFSS is cross-sectional and records past diagnoses, these
are associations, not causal effects; they plausibly reflect reverse
causation, as people often change their behaviour after a diagnosis of
heart disease. The model is suited to identifying who is likely to have
CVD, not to estimating the effect of modifying a risk factor.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

Class weighting improves recall, but it distorts the predicted
probabilities. On the out-of-fold predictions, where 9.3\% of the records
have CVD, the improved models predict an average risk of 32--36\%
(Table~\ref{tab:calibration}), and their Brier scores (0.150--0.177) are
worse than that of a constant prediction equal to the prevalence (0.084).
The raw outputs therefore must not be read as individual risks, in line
with the overestimation of risk that Carriero et al.~\cite{carriero2025}
found for imbalance corrections. After the
prior correction of Section~\ref{sec:stats}, which uses no additional data,
the mean predicted risk is 7.6--9.5\% and the Brier score falls to
0.070--0.072, better than the constant prediction. The corrected
calibration curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration}) lie close to the diagonal
for XGBoost; the MLP slightly underestimates and LR slightly overestimates
the risk in the highest-risk group. With this correction, which leaves the
AUC and the classifications unchanged, the models provide both a
classification and a usable risk estimate.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing Studies}
\label{sec:compare}

Table~\ref{tab:literature} compares the proposed method with existing
studies. A meaningful comparison requires the same dataset and an
evaluation protocol that keeps the test data unseen, so the table is
organised by dataset.

\paragraph{Same dataset (BRFSS 2015).} Deng et al.~\cite{deng} trained a
LightGBM model on the same survey data and obtained an AUC of 0.811 with
90.6\% accuracy. This accuracy was measured at the natural prevalence of
about 10\%, where a trivial classifier that always predicts ``no CVD''
already reaches 89.7\% (reference row in Table~\ref{tab:literature}), so it
mainly reflects the class imbalance; our accuracy, in contrast, is measured
on a balanced test set, where the same trivial classifier scores 50\%. On
the AUC, which is comparable across both settings, all three proposed models
in the table exceed Deng et al.: XGBoost reaches 0.840 with a recall of
82.8\%, and logistic regression and the MLP reach 0.837 and 0.838. Deng et
al.'s AUC of 0.811 lies below the lower 95\% confidence bound of all three
models (0.827--0.830; Table~\ref{tab:ci}).

\paragraph{Cleveland dataset.} Five studies in Table~\ref{tab:literature}
use the clinical Cleveland data. Applied unchanged to this dataset, as
described in Section~\ref{sec:cleveland}, the improved pipeline reaches
80.1--81.3\% accuracy and an AUC of 0.893--0.900 for SVM, XGBoost, LR and
the three DL models (the depth-limited DT, whose leaf size of 50 records was
chosen for the large BRFSS data, reaches 72.7\%). These accuracies are
slightly below the lowest published Cleveland result in the table (82.5\%)
and well below the highest (95.9\%). The variation between folds, however,
is large: the accuracy of LR on individual test folds of 59--60 patients
ranges from 71.2\% to 90.0\%, so single-split results on this dataset are
uncertain by several percentage points, and differences of a few points
between studies are within this range. Our AUC agrees closely with that of
Adekoya et al.~\cite{adekoya2025} (0.899), who emphasise strict
methodological controls.

\paragraph{Other datasets.} The remaining studies use other clinical
datasets (a CVD dataset, a Beijing hypertension cohort and the KNHANES
survey) and are listed for completeness; their results are not directly
comparable with ours.

Two factors explain why studies on clinical data report higher accuracy
than any model trained on BRFSS. First, clinical measurements carry far
more information about coronary disease than self-reported survey answers:
on Cleveland, the same pipeline that reaches an AUC of 0.84 on BRFSS
achieves an AUC of about 0.90. Second, accuracies estimated on a few dozen
test patients vary by several percentage points between random splits, as
our repeated cross-validation shows, and
several studies do not state whether resampling or feature selection was
performed before the data were split. Resampling before splitting is known
to inflate performance estimates~\cite{santos,kapoor}: when we resampled
our data before splitting them, the same HS models reached 94.7--99.8\%
accuracy instead of 57.4--70.8\%.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The BRFSS data are self-reported and cross-sectional: the target records a
past diagnosis of coronary heart disease or myocardial infarction, so the
models identify individuals who resemble patients with a history of CVD
rather than predict future events. Self-reported survey answers also carry
less information than clinical measurements, which limits the attainable
discrimination. The models were developed and tested on a single survey
year without external validation; calibration was therefore assessed only
internally, and the prior correction assumes that the prevalence in the
target population matches that of the training data. The balanced test set makes accuracy
comparable across models, but precision and F1 depend on prevalence, and
the cross-validation results indicate the values to expect in a general
population. The Cleveland evaluation used the hyper-parameters chosen for
BRFSS without re-tuning, which favours a fair comparison over the best
attainable score on that dataset.
